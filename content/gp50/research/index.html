Leaner and Meaner Amp Modelers LTTC (Lean Twin Trained Clippers) research log

This is a log of the AI-assisted research I did to improve on Valeton's SnapTone amp modelling format. This is part of the work on my custom Peppercorn firmware mod for the Valeton GP-50. The research is AI-assisted, but as always, this blog post is entirely human written.

Neural Amp Modeler (NAM) is an amazing piece of open-source technology that can be trained to reproduce the sound of any amp or distortion pedal. It is, to my ears, basically perfect, and quite lightweight to run for what it is, but it is no Doom: it's a real neural network, and there is still hardware out there that can't run it.

The Valeton GP-50 is one of those unfortunate cases, but Valeton came up with a solution: SnapTone trains a very simple architecture (EQ → clipper → EQ) to match a NAM teacher. It works remarkably well on clean amps, and trains incredibly fast, but leaves a lot to be desired for high-gain sounds.

I was already working on a firmware mod for the GP-50 (that you can install here), so naturally, the question was: can we do better?

Learning from the Real Thing

And it turns out we can! I conjured memories from my DSP and machine learning classes and tried a bunch of different methods, iterating on architecture and training methods, to get better results. The two key changes I contributed were inspired by how actual amps and distortion pedals work:

  • The clipper itself needs to be flexible, since distortion pedals use clippers with very different characteristics (diodes vs tubes etc.),
  • And emulating high-gain sounds produced with consecutive gain stages might require consecutive clippers.

You can see both of these in the final LTTC architecture.

BOGO Captures, Free IR with Every Purchase (Limit 2 per Customer)

Some of these intermediate candidate architectures look a bit unusual or nonsensical, and part of that is the constraint to have this run in the pedal, which doesn't give us that much processing power or flash firmware space to work with. In fact, storage was arguably as much of a challenge as pure processing speed in this investigation.

Having a large FIR filter at the output was also basically imposed, for two reasons:

  • Full combo amp captures have a cabinet at the end of the chain, and we know a large enough IR can capture that well, so ending the chain with it makes a lot of intuitive sense.
  • But also, it lets us run IRs after the LTTC capture for free, simply by merging that IR with the output filter. That's how I can get an LTTC capture running with an IR on the GP-50!

The final architecture is lean enough that I can run two captures at once on the pedal.

Behind the Screens

I had the LLM hold a lab notebook, but as you can see some data is still missing (e.g. notebook steps with no new architectures or scores), so I had it rescore all the architectures. We changed losses several times, and some changes were guided by my human ears, so if some trends look weird in the data, that is part of why.

The graph below presents a chronological look into the research. For any point in time, you can listen to the candidate architecture, look at its graph and metrics, and even play it live in the browser, compared to SnapTone and NAM. I can't include the NAM files directly for license reasons, but you can download them from the link and load them to play them here.

I also had it keep track of what directions were proposed by me vs the LLM, out of curiosity. This is not to say the agent couldn't have reached an equivalent or better architecture without my input, but if you're curious what AI-assisted research can look like when you don't have infinite tokens to spin up agent swarms, here's one example.

This research plus the firmware work was all done during evenings and weekends over about a month with an Anthropic Pro subscription and a ChatGPT Plus subscription, using mostly GPT Sol 6.1 and Claude Opus 5.5, for a total cost of ~$40. And I didn't brick the pedal once (although I had a few scares)!

What's next

There's still more I'd like to do on this: fuzzes are still so so hard for these architectures (see the Fuzz Factory attempts), and although I really wanted to handle amp compression and dynamics, the experiments adding a trainable compressor or any kind of state did not yield noticeable gains. You'll notice compressors and fuzzes are the two novelties Neural DSP's Neural Capture v2 brought to the table. They use a much heavier architecture for this no doubt, but I'd be curious to know how they did it, and if I can have that run on this pedal.
